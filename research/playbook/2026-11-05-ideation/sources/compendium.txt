 How judging works
     Finalist judging: each team gets 7 minutes: a 4-minute demo, then 3 minutes of questions. Many
     rooms run in parallel; each can see about 25 teams in 3 hours, so this is a first-round screen.
     Criteria (five): technicality, originality, practicality (complete and usable today), usability
     (UI/UX/DX) and "WOW factor".
     Sponsor prizes are judged from your submission materials. Booth visits are optional. With about
     100 entries per sponsor, three hours means under 2 minutes per project, so the write-up, the per-
     sponsor explanation in the form and the video do most of the work.
     Prepare for: "What inspired your project?", "What tools did you use, and why?", "What challenges
     did you solve, and how?"

 Rules that can disqualify you
     From scratch: all project work must start after hacking begins; public libraries and starter kits are
     fine. Pre-built projects only qualify through a continuity track.
     Continuity tracks (extend open source you maintain, or ship a feature): document pre-existing
     work clearly.
     Version control: commit often. "Large single commits or missing histories may be disqualified."
     AI tools allowed, with attribution: state where and how AI was used, by file. Projects built entirely
     by AI may lose eligibility. If you use a spec-driven workflow, include all spec files and prompts.
     Up to 3 partner prizes; several tracks from one partner count as one.
     Demo video: optional but strongly recommended; 2–4 minutes, at least 720p, not sped up, no
     phone recording, no AI voiceover, no music-with-text instead of talking.

 4. How deep a project has to be
 Why the bar moved between 2025 and 2026
     Sept 2025: AI coding agents were usable but limited. Claude Code launched in May 2025; GPT-5
     and Claude Opus 4.1 came out in August; Claude Sonnet 4.5 was released 29 September, the day
     after Delhi ended. Wiring five tools together in 36 hours was an achievement.
     2026: agents write most of the code (payOrRefuse said Claude wrote most of it; Fiducia ran two
     coordinating Claude Code sessions). Integration alone is no longer impressive.
     What still holds value: real devices and real-world testing, new mechanisms or hard maths, and
     your own data and proof.

    Target: a Level 2 product with one Level 3 component: one part that is hard, real and proven.
    Teams at this scale usually fail by building too much and demoing too little, not by aiming too low.

 Time budget
     Hacking Thu 9 PM → Sat 9 AM = 36 hours. After sleep (6–8 h each), food and workshops: about
     22–26 working hours per person; a team of 4 has about 90–100 person-hours.
     AI speeds up code 2–3×, but not the bottlenecks: sponsor SDK surprises, testnets, device testing,
     recording.
     Split: 60% core build, 15% integration fixes, 15% demo/video/write-up, 10% buffer.
     Feature freeze around hour 26 (Friday ~11 PM); last 10 hours for testing, measuring and recording.

 Finish-line checklist
     Live demo, no hard-coded values
     Each sponsor's feature essential and showable in under 30 seconds
     Contracts deployed, explorer links in the write-up
     A few dozen tests on the core logic
     One number you measured yourselves
     Write-up: problem, how it's built, honest limits; AI usage documented
     3–4 minute video meeting the rules

 5. Fiducia: our earlier project
 Where it came from
     Built by Dhruv and Vedant for Superteam Germany's "Build an MVP with Solana at WHU" (WHU
     hackathon 2026). Took 3rd of 17 ($500 USDG; 1st Shreyansh Tripathi, 2nd Guido Andreini). Judged
     on a useful idea, a working prototype, a clear role for Solana and potential to grow.
     Repo: github.com/Mulaydm10/solana-hackathon (411 commits). Live: fiducia-orpin.vercel.app.
     Later extended for the peaq Machine Economy track (Crypto World's Fair 2026), with a 12-slide
     deck, 3-page technical brief and 3-page go-to-market plan (12 Oct).
     Conceptual ancestor: your Crusoe-track win at RAISE (a trust-and-safety control plane for a robot
     fleet).

 What it does
 "Fiducia controls how AI agents spend money." One Anchor program (deal_escrow) enforces on-
 chain mandates: per-payment and total caps, approved payees, stages bound to a plan hash, expiry,
 one-click revoke. Pieces: data marketplace (assessed, encrypted, escrow), paid services (x402, "no
 answer, no charge"), agent teams, an MCP server with 17 tools, a demand board, and the peaq
 machine economy (a simulated delivery robot buying proven kWh from three charging pads, an MCR-
 style credit score, parametric downtime insurance, Fiducia Insure over x402).

 Strengths
     Unusually deep engineering: 636 tests (program 97, agents 325, web 179, MCP 35), 2,000
     randomized attack-search runs with 0 invariant violations, the deployed binary verified byte for
     byte.
     Proof shown, not claimed: a /proof page and explorer links; an over-cap payment refused with
     OverPerTxCap.
     An honest-limits section; a named first customer and pricing (1.5%, or $149/month + 1%); a
     concrete reason for Solana (card fees vs about 0.09%).
     The peaq version was focused (one job, one customer), mapped to every track area and reused
     one escrow for insurance.

 Weaknesses we found
     The live demo was broken when checked (catalogue RPC failure, /hire server error, /machines
     intermittent); the robot's mandate expired on 30 Oct; the 30-minute clock ran on an "always-on
     Mac".
     The robot's "small business" lost money: $9.00 earned vs $15.77 spent on energy.
     Grades in the deck and on the live page disagreed (robot BB 57 vs AAA 100; pad A BBB 61 vs NR
     24); a money-losing robot scored AAA.
     Insurance moral hazard: an unrated pad pays 0.20 for 1.00 cover and could stop heartbeats
     deliberately to collect.
     The pad signs its own meter reading; peaq events are self-reported (trust level 0).

 The test every idea must pass
    Ask "can't people just do X?" If a cheap, obvious X exists and mostly works, the idea is weak.
    Strong problems are ones where the obvious fix requires trusting someone untrustworthy, doesn't
    scale, can't be done by the people affected, or needs information that doesn't exist yet.

 A. DawaBox (with the proofread fixes)
 Problem: prescription-only medicines are routinely sold without valid prescriptions; fake prescriptions
 and non-existent doctors are common.
  1. The doctor signs a QR on the prescription; the kiosk checks it against the doctor's ENSv2 subname
     (council can revoke; only the doctor can write).
  2. A vision model on 0G reads the printed text, matches it to the QR and checks drug conflicts:
     dispense, refuse or escalate.
  3. For a restricted drug, the agent asks the on-call pharmacist for a fresh World ID approval (Agent
     Pass); the patient's anonymous World ID enforces a monthly per-person limit.
  4. The motor dispenses; a fingerprint of the signed QR content (not the photo) is recorded on-chain
     so reuse is refused.

 The control test
 You ran a generic prompt in a fresh session. It researched the prize page but not the winners. Its top
 pick ("Leash", agent spending limits) was the most crowded winning idea; "Sentinel" (ML dynamic-fee
 hook) duplicated OniBlock; "Brainbank" targeted 0G Storage, which no Mumbai 0G track rewards, and
 missed the Arc Studio rule; "Lease.eth" used an off-chain resolver that may not satisfy ENSv2's
 "central" rule; it missed World's $5,000 track. It did contribute: Lease.eth as a fresh idea, Arkiv's Sub0
 data (32 submissions from about 150 hackers), and the logistics (start coding at the event, video
 rules). Conclusion: the winner analysis, not special creativity, is what improved the ideas.

 NetraPay, and why it was dropped

 safe stop if 0G is unreachable.

 LeakMeter (live candidate)
 Problem: a wallet's RPC provider sees every address and token you look up, plus your IP and timing,
 so it can link your wallets and infer holdings without any transaction. Most people can't run their own
 node. (In 2022 ConsenSys said Infura collects IP and wallet addresses on transactions; from memory,
 verify before citing.)
  1. Measure: record your own test wallets' sessions; show step by step what the provider can infer
     ("after 2 minutes it links these 3 wallets"); a leak score.
  2. Fix: route lookups through EF's Anon-RPC.
  3. Fix: mirror public data wallets need (token lists, prices, address labels) into Arkiv.
  4. Prove: same session again, score drops (e.g. 3 linked wallets → 0).
 Sponsors: EF Anon-RPC ($2.5k / $1.5k / $1k), Arkiv Mirror ($3k / $1k + $500), optional ENSv2
 privacy-score records (up to $3k). Why it fits: own measurement at the centre (like TARE and x402
 Guard), no similar winner found, no hardware, suits your ML/data background. Risk: less flashy, so the
 live before-and-after panel must make the leak feel personal.

 8. First-hackathon advice and how to decide
     Feeling unsure at your first big hackathon is normal. Reading the projects and briefs yourself is the
     right instinct: you notice things an AI doesn't.
     Give yourself a deadline: pick an idea by around 10 PM tonight. A decent idea built well beats a
     perfect idea still in your head on Friday.
     Choose the problem that genuinely bugs you; that excitement carries you through 36 hours.
     Test it once on a sponsor or mentor. If they lean in and start asking questions, that's your answer.
